Research report · human temporal cortex transcriptomics

A reproducible Alzheimer’s disease transcriptomic signature across three platforms, and what it is made of

Suraj Rathi, BTech Life Sciences

Abstract

Background. Transcriptomic studies of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) often report different gene lists depending on the technology, brain region and cohort studied. Whether a shared disease signal exists across platforms, and whether it reflects changes inside cells or changes in which cells are present, is not settled.

Methods. I analysed three human temporal-cortex datasets with platform-appropriate models: an Illumina microarray of the middle temporal gyrus (GSE132903; 97 AD, 98 controls; limma), bulk RNA-seq of the lateral temporal lobe (GSE104704; 12 AD, 10 age-matched controls; DESeq2) and donor-level pseudobulk of single-nucleus RNA-seq from the Seattle Alzheimer’s Disease Brain Cell Atlas (SEA-AD; 10 dementia, 10 controls; DESeq2). I compared fold changes across platforms, validated every result with permutation, covariate, outlier and leave-one-out tests, resolved the single-nucleus data by cell type, trained classifiers on a 31-gene panel, and studied the panel as a protein-interaction and co-expression network.

Results. Fold changes correlated positively across all dataset pairs (r = 0.26–0.41; 13,717 shared genes). Among genes significant in two datasets, 93–100% changed in the same direction (100 of 101 microarray/RNA-seq genes; 73 of 73 microarray/SEA-AD genes), far beyond chance. Neuronal and neuropeptide genes (VGF, SST, NPTX2, SNAP25) decreased and glial genes (GFAP, GJA1, VIM) increased. A classifier trained on the microarray identified AD brains in the independent RNA-seq cohort with AUC 0.90–0.95. Cell-type resolution showed that glial genes rose in tissue (median log2FC +1.68) but barely changed per cell (+0.09), as somatostatin interneurons halved and astrocytes expanded in dementia. Similarly, apparent co-expression rewiring among the panel genes (108 of 465 gene pairs) almost vanished (3 of 465) after accounting for cell composition. The neuronal repressor REST was the top predicted regulator of the down-regulated genes.

Conclusions. The AD transcriptomic signal is reproducible across microarray, bulk and single-nucleus RNA-seq and across temporal-cortex regions. Much of it is a signature of cell composition, namely neuron loss and gliosis, rather than changed expression per cell. Separating the two requires models that treat brain tissue as a mixture of interacting cell populations.

1Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is the most common cause of dementia. It is marked by amyloid plaques, tau tangles, synaptic loss and the death of specific neuronal populations, accompanied by reactive astrocytes and microglia. Gene-expression profiling of post-mortem brain has been a central tool for studying these processes, and large datasets now exist from microarrays, bulk RNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq.1–4

Two problems limit how these data can be interpreted. First, studies disagree. Differentially expressed gene lists from different technologies, brain regions and cohorts often overlap only partly, which raises the question of whether a shared AD signal exists at all or whether each study mainly reflects its own platform. Second, bulk tissue is a mixture. When neurons die and glia proliferate, the expression of every neuron- or glia-specific gene shifts in bulk measurements even if no cell changes its own expression. A tissue-level signature can therefore look like gene regulation when it is in fact a change in cell composition. Single-nucleus atlases such as SEA-AD now make it possible to test this directly.3,4

This project addresses both problems with three datasets from the human temporal cortex, a region affected early in AD, measured with three different technologies. It had four aims:

  • Reproducibility: test whether AD-associated expression changes agree in direction and magnitude across microarray, bulk RNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq, and validate that agreement against explicit null models.
  • Composition vs regulation: use cell-type-resolved single-nucleus data to separate changes in cell proportions from changes in expression within cells.
  • Transferability: test whether a classifier trained on one platform can identify AD tissue on another without retraining.
  • Systems view: analyse a panel of core genes as a network, asking which genes hold it together, whether its co-expression structure changes in AD, and which transcription factors may regulate it.

2Materials and methods

2.1 Datasets

RNA-seq controls were the age-matched “Old” group; the young group was excluded. GEO provides GSE104704 only as size-factor-normalised values. Because every value in a sample equals an integer count divided by the same size factor, dividing each sample by its smallest non-zero value recovered the raw integer counts exactly (maximum deviation 2 × 10⁻⁹). For SEA-AD, 20 donors were grouped by cognitive status (dementia vs no dementia). Raw UMIs from layers["UMIs"] were summed over all nuclei of each donor to form a whole-tissue pseudobulk.

2.2 Differential expression

Microarray probes were mapped to genes with illuminaHumanv4.db, keeping the probe with the highest mean intensity per gene (a choice independent of the AD/control comparison). Expression was modelled with limma (~ condition, control as the explicit reference level).5 RNA-seq and SEA-AD counts were analysed with DESeq2 (~ condition, control as reference), and fold changes were shrunk with apeglm.6,7 Genes were called significant at Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p (padj) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5.

2.3 Cross-platform comparison

Results were merged by gene symbol. For each dataset pair I computed the Pearson correlation of log2FC over all shared genes and over genes significant in both, and the fraction of genes significant in both that changed in the same direction. A 33-gene candidate panel of neuronal, neuropeptide and glial genes, consistently changed across the datasets (31 measured on the microarray; CAMK2A and PENK have no probe), was used for focused analyses.

2.4 Validation

Each dataset was checked for label correctness, contrast direction (known AD markers), covariate balance (age, sex), covariate-adjusted models, p-value calibration, label-permutation nulls (200 for microarray, 20 for the sequencing datasets), principal-component outliers, split-half reproducibility and leave-one-sample-out influence. The comparison was tested against hypergeometric and binomial nulls, a gene-shuffle null (1,000 shuffles), bootstrap confidence intervals and removal of the most influential samples. Panel selection bias was assessed by comparing the panel with random gene sets. Every check had a pre-set PASS / WARN / FAIL rule.

2.5 Cell-type-resolved pseudobulk

SEA-AD subclasses were grouped into 10 classes (excitatory IT, deep-layer excitatory, Pvalb/Chandelier, Sst/Sst Chodl, Vip/Lamp5/Sncg/Pax6, astrocytes, microglia/immune, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, vascular cells). Raw UMIs were summed per donor within each class, and summing the classes back reproduced the whole-tissue pseudobulk exactly. Each class with at least 4 donors per group (≥ 20 nuclei each) was analysed with DESeq2 and apeglm, a 20-shuffle permutation null, and a refit without the two donors with the most glia-heavy sampling.

2.6 Classifier

L2-regularised logistic regression (glmnet, λ by inner cross-validation) and random forests (500 trees) were trained on the 31 panel genes in the microarray.8,9 Each gene was z-scored within its own dataset, a label-free step that makes microarray intensities and sequencing counts comparable. Performance was estimated by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV), with scaling fitted inside each fold. A decision threshold giving ≥ 0.85 sensitivity was fixed on the LOO predictions. The final models and threshold were then applied without change to RNA-seq and SEA-AD. AUCs were computed with pROC.10 A learning curve used stratified subsamples of 20–100% of the microarray (10 repeats each).

2.7 Network analysis

Functional-association edges among the panel genes were taken from STRING v12.5 (combined score ≥ 0.4).11 Hubs were ranked by degree, betweenness and eigenvector centrality. Modularity was compared with 1,000 degree-preserving rewirings, communities were detected with Louvain,12 bridging was measured by the participation coefficient,13 and robustness by random vs targeted node removal.14 Differential co-expression compared Spearman networks in AD and controls: per-gene connectivity change Δk with 2,000 label permutations, and per-edge Fisher z-tests. It was repeated after regressing out marker-based proxies for neuron, astrocyte, microglia, oligodendrocyte and Sst-interneuron abundance. Signed WGCNA was run on the 5,000 most variable microarray genes plus the panel.15 Module coherence was compared with 2,000 expression-matched random gene sets. Transcription-factor enrichment used TRRUST v2 with one-sided Fisher’s exact tests.16

2.8 Software

Analyses used R 4.4 (limma, DESeq2, apeglm, WGCNA, glmnet, randomForest, pROC, hdf5r, ggplot2) and Python 3 (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, NetworkX, scikit-learn, matplotlib). Random seeds were fixed and every script records its session information.

In the microarray, all canonical AD markers changed in the expected direction: VGF −1.23, NPTX2 −1.08, SST −0.81 and BDNF −0.57 decreased, while GFAP +1.05, CD44 +0.67 and SERPINA3 +0.69 increased (all padj < 0.01). In RNA-seq, VGF was the most significant gene (−2.37) and all ten neuronal markers decreased. The glial increase was weaker in this small cohort (GFAP +0.45, not significant).

The SEA-AD whole-tissue result was shaped by which cells each donor contributed. Dementia donors yielded fewer nuclei with relatively more astrocytes. In donor H20.33.038 only 26% of nuclei were neurons (Figure 5). Up-regulated genes dominated, and the most significant genes were mostly non-coding and vascular transcripts. Only VIM and GJA1 among the panel genes reached significance.

The agreement in direction across platforms is therefore robust. The SEA-AD whole-tissue gene list is not, and the panel’s consistency mostly reflects the general replication of significant genes rather than anything specific to the panel.

3.4 Much of the signature is cell composition

Within each cell type, glial genes barely changed even though they rose strongly in tissue: GFAP +1.33 in tissue vs +0.03 in astrocytes, VIM +2.04 vs −0.04, GJA1 +2.73 vs +0.43, TREM2 +1.22 vs +0.14 in microglia. SST fell −1.46 in tissue but only −0.35 inside Sst neurons. In contrast, CARTPT (−1.14), NPTX2 (−0.74) and CHGB (−0.57) fell about as much inside excitatory neurons as in tissue, which marks them as candidate cell-intrinsic changes (not significant with 10 donors per group). Composition itself shifted: in dementia, Sst interneurons halved (p = 0.002), Pvalb neurons decreased (p = 0.03) and astrocytes increased about 2.8-fold (p = 0.03).

3.6 The gene network: modular, hub-dependent and composition-driven

The panel formed a STRING network of 131 edges with strong modularity (Q = 0.23 vs −0.02 ± 0.03 for random graphs, z = 8.6). The top hubs were SNAP25, GFAP, NEFL, SST and CAMK2A, and the first four kept their rank using experimental and database evidence alone. Removing the highest-betweenness genes broke the network apart after 7 removals, compared with about 15 for random removal.

In the microarray, co-expression among panel genes changed for 108 of 465 gene pairs in AD, mostly neuronal genes becoming more tightly coupled. After the neuron and Sst-interneuron abundance proxies, both strongly reduced in AD (p < 10⁻⁴), were regressed out, only 3 pairs changed and no gene’s connectivity change survived FDR correction (Figure 26). Unsupervised WGCNA placed the neuronal genes in three modules that decrease in AD (r = −0.38 to −0.41) and the glial genes in modules that increase (strongest r = +0.51). The data therefore support one neuronal axis set against a glial axis rather than three independent modules (Figure 27).

TRRUST enrichment identified the neuronal repressor REST as the top regulator of the panel (targets VGF, CHGB, TAC1, CARTPT; FDR 3 × 10⁻⁴) and of the genes consistently down in AD (adding BDNF, PCSK1, TUBB3; FDR 2 × 10⁻⁵). REST expression was higher in AD in all three datasets: microarray +0.15 (padj 2 × 10⁻⁶), RNA-seq +0.16 and SEA-AD +0.96 (not significant).

4Discussion

This project shows that Alzheimer’s disease leaves a reproducible transcriptomic signature in the human temporal cortex. Three technologies, two cortical regions and three cohorts agree on its direction: neuronal and neuropeptide genes decrease and glial genes increase. The agreement is far stronger than chance, survives covariate adjustment and sample removal, and lets a classifier trained on microarrays identify AD tissue in an independent RNA-seq cohort.

The second finding explains why the signature is so reproducible. Much of it is a signature of cell composition. When single-nucleus data were resolved by cell type, the large tissue-level rise in glial genes almost disappeared inside astrocytes and microglia, and the fall in SST was mostly explained by the loss of Sst interneurons. The network analysis led to the same conclusion independently: extensive apparent rewiring of co-expression in AD vanished once neuron and interneuron abundance were taken into account. Neuron loss and gliosis are central features of AD pathology, so this is real disease biology. It is, however, a different claim from saying that AD changes how individual cells regulate these genes.

A smaller set of genes points to cell-intrinsic change. CARTPT, NPTX2 and CHGB decreased inside excitatory neurons by about as much as in tissue. NPTX2 has been linked to synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline in AD, and the enrichment of REST targets among the down-regulated genes suggests a possible regulatory mechanism. REST protects ageing neurons and is reported to be lost from the nucleus in AD,17 so the higher REST mRNA seen here should be treated as a hypothesis to test rather than a mechanism.

Limitations

  • Panel selection. The 33-gene panel was chosen for consistency across these same datasets. Its consistency and the RNA-seq classifier result are therefore optimistic, and an independent fourth cohort is needed for a clean test.
  • Sample size. RNA-seq (n = 22) and SEA-AD (n = 20) are small. SEA-AD whole-tissue and per-cell-type gene lists did not pass all validation tests and are reported as exploratory.
  • Region and labels. The RNA-seq cohort sampled the lateral temporal lobe rather than the middle temporal gyrus. SEA-AD groups are defined by cognitive status, and half of its controls carry intermediate or high AD pathology.
  • Tissue type. All samples are post-mortem brain. The classifier separates AD from control tissue and is not a diagnostic test for living patients.
  • Network evidence. STRING includes text-mining evidence, which favours well-studied genes.

Future work

  • Validate the signature and classifier in an independent cohort (for example GSE5281 or ROSMAP), with genes selected from a single training dataset.
  • Extend the cell-type analysis to the full SEA-AD cohort to give per-cell changes adequate power.
  • Estimate cell proportions in the bulk datasets by deconvolution and model composition and regulation jointly.

5Conclusion

Alzheimer’s disease produces a transcriptomic signature in the temporal cortex that replicates across microarray, bulk RNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq. Much of this signature is the footprint of a changing cell population: neurons, especially somatostatin interneurons, are lost and glia expand. A smaller set of neuronal genes, possibly under REST control, shows signs of change within cells. Treating brain tissue as a mixture of interacting cell populations, rather than as a single expression profile, is necessary to tell these two processes apart.

Data and code availability

Microarray and bulk RNA-seq data are available from NCBI GEO (GSE132903, GSE104704). Single-nucleus data are from the Seattle Alzheimer’s Disease Brain Cell Atlas. Interaction and regulatory data are from STRING v12.5 and TRRUST v2. All analysis scripts are in the project directory: differential expression, comparison, validation, cell-type and classifier scripts in analysis/, and network scripts in network/.
